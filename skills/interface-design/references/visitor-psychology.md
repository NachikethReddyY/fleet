# Visitor Psychology

Read this when layout familiarity, novelty balance, persuasion grouping, or decision justification is at stake. It covers how visitors judge an interface before they reason about it.

Model visitors as three systems in one person, evaluated in order. This is a simplification for design decisions, not neuroscience:

1. **Safety** votes first: is this familiar and safe to use?
2. **Feeling** votes second: is this interesting enough to stay?
3. **Reason** votes last: can I justify saying yes?

A design that loses the first vote rarely gets judged on the other two.

## Safety first: match mental models

Visitors arrive with a blueprint built from every interface they have used before: logo top-left, navigation near it, footer at the bottom, controls where the platform puts them. The safety system uses that blueprint to avoid thinking hard. When something expected is missing or moved, it reads the difference as risk and leaves rather than searching.

- Keep structural placement conventional: identity, navigation, content order, and footer where the domain puts them.
- Never relocate a found control for theoretical ergonomics without evidence visitors still find it. A mobile menu moved to the thumb zone failed in practice because visitors looked where menus live.
- Earn creativity inside the structure, not from the structure. Unfamiliar skeletons must prove findability with a focused check, not an argument.

## Feeling second: predictable structure, surprising details

The feeling system wants small pleasant surprises. An interface that lets safety decide everything becomes so predictable that visitors get bored and bounce. The fix is MAYA — most advanced yet acceptable: hold the structure steady, then break tiny patterns on purpose.

- Keep one predictable skeleton; spend novelty on small details: an unusual-but-legible hover response, an image that scales slightly on scroll, a transition with character.
- Each surprise must be small, pleasant, and non-blocking. If it moves layout, hides content, delays the task, or startles, it scares the safety system and fails MAYA.
- Prefer one or two restrained microinteractions per view over a dramatic effect. Frequent actions stay fast and calm; respect reduced-motion settings.

## Reason last: hand over chunked justification

Feeling usually decides before reason finishes. Reason then looks for evidence that the decision was smart. Working memory holds about three or four items at once, so a long scrolling list of benefits is dropped before the visitor reaches the action.

- Group proof into three or four chunks with visible labels, the way a phone number splits ten digits into three groups.
- Show differences, not repetitions. For tiers or plans, state each level as everything in the previous level plus its additions, with categories instead of one repeated long list.
- Place the strongest two or three reasons near the decision point. Supporting detail stays discoverable but never competes with the choice.
- Keep comparison scannable: aligned attributes, one reading direction, no needle-in-haystack hunting across repeated paragraphs.

## Gate

- Structural placement matches domain expectations; any relocation has passing findability evidence.
- The skeleton is conventional while one or two details supply restrained delight.
- No surprise moves content, blocks input, or ignores reduced motion.
- Reasons to act are grouped in threes or fours, show differences rather than repeats, and sit near the decision.
- A visitor can state what this offers, why it is safe, and what to do next without re-reading the page.
