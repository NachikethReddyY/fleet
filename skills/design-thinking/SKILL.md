# Design Thinking

Turn an ambiguous challenge into the next evidence-producing iteration. Begin with people rather than a preferred solution, separate observations from assumptions, and move non-linearly among Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test as evidence changes the problem.

Read [the field guide](references/field-guide.md) when choosing research methods, writing the working brief, or preparing an interview, ideation session, prototype, or test.

## Establish the challenge

Capture the minimum shared context before selecting a stage:

- the product, service, process, environment, or business model in scope;
- the people affected and the outcome they are trying to achieve;
- the observed difficulty or opportunity;
- existing evidence, known constraints, and protected behavior;
- the decision this iteration must inform.

Label claims as **evidence**, **inference**, or **assumption**. Do not present invented personas, imagined quotes, or untested preferences as user research. Ask for missing product meaning when different answers would materially change the work; otherwise state a reasonable assumption and continue.

## Choose the next learning stage

Select the earliest stage whose missing evidence blocks a useful decision. Do not force every request through all five stages.

1. **Empathize** when user behavior, needs, emotions, context, or pain points are unclear. Choose the smallest suitable research method and record what users do, think, and feel across relevant touchpoints.
2. **Define** when research exists but the core problem is fuzzy. Synthesize observations into insights, identify the user need, and write a specific problem statement that does not prescribe a solution.
3. **Ideate** when the problem is supported but candidate approaches are too narrow or premature. Diverge first, group related ideas, then compare the strongest concepts for desirability, feasibility, and viability.
4. **Prototype** when a concept contains an important untested assumption. Build the lowest-cost representation that lets the intended user encounter the relevant behavior or experience.
5. **Test** when a prototype can answer a decision. Observe users, collect specific feedback, and decide whether to refine, revisit an earlier stage, or stop the concept.

Stages may run in parallel, repeat, or move backward. A stage is complete only when it produces evidence or a concrete artifact that informs the current decision.

## Run the loop

Maintain one compact working brief using the template in the field guide. At each iteration:

1. Name the decision and the riskiest relevant assumption.
2. Choose one stage and one method capable of reducing that uncertainty.
3. Define the artifact or observation the method will produce.
4. Perform or prepare the work without expanding the product scope.
5. Update the brief with evidence, changed beliefs, and the next decision.

When research requires access to real users or systems that is unavailable, produce a research or test plan and mark the resulting claims unverified. Do not simulate evidence.

## Decision rules

- **Desirability:** Does this address a demonstrated human need or meaningful experience?
- **Feasibility:** Can it be delivered with plausible technology, operations, skills, and time?
- **Viability:** Can it create and sustain the intended value within the relevant model and constraints?

Use the three filters to expose tradeoffs, not to fabricate numeric certainty. Reject or revise a concept that clearly fails one filter. When evidence is incomplete, name what would resolve the uncertainty.

Prefer learning value over fidelity in prototypes. Test the riskiest assumption before polishing adjacent behavior. Treat user feedback as evidence about the problem and concept, not as an instruction to implement every request.

## Deliverable

Return or maintain a working brief that makes the current state explicit:

- challenge, users, and intended outcome;
- evidence, inferences, and assumptions;
- insight, user need, and problem statement;
- candidate ideas and the selection rationale;
- prototype and the assumption it tests;
- test method, observed feedback, and decision criteria;
- next iteration or stop decision.

Match the detail to the request. For a narrow question, provide only the relevant section plus the next useful action. For a full workshop or project, use the complete template and preserve a visible chain from evidence to decision.
