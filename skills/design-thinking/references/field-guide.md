## Empathize methods

Choose a method for the question, not because it is familiar.

| Need to learn | Suitable methods | Useful output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behavior in context | Observation, contextual inquiry, immersive experience | Behaviors, workarounds, environmental constraints |
| Motivations and emotions | Semi-structured interviews | Goals, language, tensions, mental models |
| Journey and touchpoints | Journey mapping from research | Actions, thoughts, feelings, pain points, opportunities |
| Recurring quantitative pattern | Product or operational analytics | Frequency, sequence, segments, drop-offs |
| Difference between alternatives | Prototype comparison or A/B test | Behavioral preference under controlled conditions |
| Shared understanding of a user group | Evidence-backed empathy map or persona | Synthesized patterns with sources and confidence |

For interviews, ask about recent real behavior before hypothetical preferences. Useful prompts include:

- “Tell me about the last time you tried to ___.”
- “What happened before and after that?”
- “What was most difficult or uncertain?”
- “What did you do instead?”
- “How did that affect you?”

Avoid leading questions, solution pitches, and asking users to predict whether they would use an imagined product.

## Define the problem

Move from evidence to a problem statement through three linked artifacts:

1. **Insight:** a meaningful pattern or tension supported by observations.
2. **User need:** the outcome or condition the user needs, expressed independently of a feature.
3. **Problem statement:** a clear, actionable challenge joining the user, need, and context.

Useful form:

> `<user or group>` needs a way to `<need or outcome>` when `<context>` because `<supported insight>`.

Optionally turn it into an ideation prompt:

> How might we help `<user>` achieve `<outcome>` in `<context>` without `<important constraint or failure>`?

A strong statement is human-centered, grounded in evidence, narrow enough to act on, broad enough to permit multiple solutions, and free of a prescribed implementation.

## Ideate and select

Separate divergence from evaluation:

1. Generate multiple approaches without filtering them immediately.
2. Group concepts by mechanism, experience, or problem addressed.
3. Identify the strongest or most distinct idea in each group.
4. Compare them using desirability, feasibility, and viability.
5. Select a concept, combine compatible ideas, or identify evidence needed before selection.

Mind maps are useful when relationships among ideas matter. Avoid choosing by novelty alone or collapsing to the first technically easy idea.

Use a qualitative comparison unless real data supports scoring:

## Prototype for learning

Start with the question the prototype must answer. Choose the cheapest form that creates a credible encounter with the relevant behavior:

- paper sketch or storyboard for sequence and comprehension;
- clickable wireframe for navigation and interaction;
- role-play or service walkthrough for people-and-process experiences;
- concierge or Wizard-of-Oz simulation for service value before automation;
- physical model for size, handling, ergonomics, or spatial fit;
- digital simulation, AR, or VR for an environment or process that is costly to build.

State what is intentionally absent so fidelity is not mistaken for completeness. Do not build production infrastructure to test a value or usability assumption that a smaller artifact can answer.

## Test for a decision

Define the decision criteria before observing the test. Give participants a realistic goal rather than instructions that reveal the intended path. Observe behavior first, then ask:

- What worked or felt useful?
- What was confusing, difficult, or unwanted?
- What did you expect to happen?
- What was missing for the task?
- What would you change, and why?

Record behavior, quotations, and interpretation separately. Note the participant and context without claiming that a small sample represents everyone.

End with one explicit result:

- **Refine** when the problem and concept remain supported but the implementation needs change.
- **Revisit a stage** when feedback changes the understanding of users, the problem, or the option set.
- **Stop** when the core assumption is contradicted or the concept cannot plausibly satisfy a required innovation filter.
